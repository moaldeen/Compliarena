Q1 — Structural Elements & Defaults Layout top-to-bottom: status bar spacer → close button → app icon + headline → feature list → plan selector → billing summary → CTA → compliance footer. Mandatory elements (cannot be omitted under Google Play policy): Price and billing period for each plan — required disclosure Auto-renewal language keyed to the selected plan — required disclosure A way to cancel or leave the screen — required; implemented as the X button Links to Privacy Policy and Terms of Service — required Optional elements and reasoning: The app icon and tagline are decorative but serve as brand anchors; a cold paywall with no identity cue increases distrust and drop-off, so they stay The "Billed today" summary row is technically redundant with the plan cards but removes the last moment of price ambiguity right before the tap — worth the single line of space The savings badge ("Save 67%") is a conversion aid, not a compliance requirement; it stays because it directly supports the annual plan selection without misrepresenting the offer Defaults: Yearly is pre-selected. This is deliberate — annual plans have better LTV, and a pre-selected state is permitted under Google Play policy as long as the price is clearly displayed (which it is). The user can freely switch to monthly before committing. Q2 — Contextual & Manifest Tracking The manifest provided three environmental signals: category (photo editing), platform (Google Play / Android), compliance requirement (Google Play subscriptions policy). Photo editing category directly shaped the visual tone. Photo editing apps compete on perceived premium quality — the dark gradient, the purple-pink accent palette, and the AI-feature framing all signal a high-end creative tool rather than a utility. A productivity app would call for a lighter, more neutral presentation. Google Play / Android platform shaped two structural decisions: (1) the compliance copy explicitly names "Google Play subscriptions" as the management path — this is the correct and required pointer for Android, not "App Store" or a generic URL; (2) the close button is present and functional from the first frame. Google Play policy prohibits paywalls that trap users — a dismissible entry point is not optional. Google Play subscriptions policy was the most constraining signal. It mandated: exact price per billing cycle visible before any commitment tap, auto-renewal disclosure using specific timing language ("at least 24 hours before renewal"), and the cancel pathway reference. None of these were softened or deferred to a post-tap screen. The compliance footer updates dynamically when the user switches plans precisely because showing the wrong renewal amount for the active selection would be a policy violation. What I did not change based on the manifest: the device frame simulation itself, which is a rendering context rather than a product requirement, and the choice of icon or copy, which fall outside manifest scope. Q3 — Design Intent & Interaction Balances Three core tensions governed every decision: 1. Transparency vs. cognitive load. Google Play policy demands full disclosure, but walls of legal text kill conversion. The resolution: compliance copy is present and complete but visually de-emphasized (10px, 30% white opacity) while the price — the most decision-relevant fact — is rendered at the largest non-headline type size on the screen. Users who need the legal detail can read it; users who have already decided are not slowed down by it. 2. Choice architecture vs. neutrality. Presenting two plans risks analysis paralysis. The yearly plan is pre-selected and carries a "Best Value" badge and savings callout. This nudges without deceiving — the monthly option is equally reachable, clearly priced, and one tap away. The nudge is price-justified (the math is accurate) and policy-compliant (no false urgency, no countdown timers, no dark patterns). 3. Exit ease vs. conversion.
