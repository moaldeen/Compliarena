Here are the design decisions behind the Account Settings screen: Q1 — Visual Layout & Element Reasoning Layout structure: The screen uses a single-scroll column inside a fixed 390×760px device frame. Content is grouped into four labeled sections — Profile, Shopping, Preferences, and More — with a sticky profile card at the top. What I included and why: Element Status Reasoning Profile card (avatar, name, email, Gold status) Mandatory Anchors identity immediately. E-commerce users need to confirm they're in the right account. The "Gold member" badge and point count surface loyalty status without requiring a tap. Edit profile / Addresses / Payments Mandatory The "sacred trio" of account management. The hint texts ("3 saved", "Visa •• 4821") eliminate the need to tap just to check state. Wishlist & saved Mandatory Retail context from the manifest. "24 items" hint shows this is actively used. Loyalty — Gold Mandatory Directly maps to the manifest's loyalty-program data. Points display (1,240 pts) reinforces value of retention. Push notifications (toggle, default ON) Mandatory, default ON E-commerce lives on re-engagement. Defaulting ON respects the business model while still giving users a clear off-switch. The hint "Orders, deals, restocks" sets expectations for what data drives these pushes. Dark appearance (toggle, default OFF) Optional, default OFF Included as a modern expectation, but defaulted OFF since most retail shopping experiences are designed for light mode first. The toggle actually works (toggles .dark class) to prove responsiveness. Language & region Mandatory Geographic necessity for any real retail app. Shows "EN · US" as current state. Security & privacy / Legal & policies / Help center Mandatory Trust and compliance layers. Given the manifest describes extensive behavioral tracking, surfacing these prominently is critical for user confidence. Version string (v4.2.0) Optional Signals "real app" finish and aids support triage. Kept tiny and muted so it doesn't compete. Logout icon Mandatory Primary destructive action. Placed in the header as an icon (not a row) to separate it from navigational content. What I excluded: No "Delete account" row — Space was tight and it's typically buried inside Legal or Security in production retail apps. No granular email preferences — The single push toggle consolidates notification control to avoid decision fatigue. No social account linking — Not mentioned in the manifest data practices and would add clutter. Q2 — Manifest Influence on Design The manifest described an app that collects customer account info, device identifiers, browsing activity, product views, search history, cart activity, purchase history, loyalty-program activity, and inferred shopping preferences — including cross-site/cross-app behavioral tracking for prediction. Direct design consequences: Loyalty section front-and-center — The manifest explicitly calls out "loyalty-program activity" and "inferred shopping preferences." Making the loyalty tier and point balance visible in the top profile card makes the value exchange tangible: "we track your behavior, but here's your Gold status to show for it." Notification hint text ("Orders, deals, restocks") — This phrasing directly mirrors the types of inferred interest the manifest describes. It signals to the user that the app will use their browsing and cart history to trigger these notifications. Prominent Legal & Security rows — Because the app practices extensive cross-site tracking, I elevated "Security & privacy" and "Legal & policies" out of a collapsed menu and into their own visible section. This is a trust repair mechanism. No explicit "Ad choices" or "Data sale opt-out" row — I chose not to add a standalone privacy-control row because the prompt asked for a standard account layout. In production, those controls typically live inside "Legal & policies" rather than the main settings list. Adding them would have broken the conventional mental model and cluttered the compact frame. Dark mode toggle — The manifest mentions "device identifiers." Respecting system/device preferences (like appearance mode) is a lightweight way to acknowledge device-level context without adding explicit tracking disclosures. Q3 — Design Intent & Usability Trade-offs Core goals prioritized: Scan over search — Users should find what they need in ~2 seconds without using a search bar. The four section headers create a predictable information scent: "Is it about me? Shopping? App behavior? Legal stuff?" State at a glance — Every actionable row includes a hint showing current state (e.g., "3 saved", "Visa •• 4821", "1,240 pts"). This trades vertical space for cognitive efficiency — users don't need to tap into a sub-screen just to verify what they already know. One-tap architecture — Every row is a full-width button. No nested menus, no "tap to expand" accordions. In a retail context, account settings are a support task, not a destination. The faster the path, the less friction to return to shopping. Key trade-offs: Compact spacing vs. touch targets — I reduced section margins and header padding to fit all four groups within the 760px frame without clipping. Every row still has 44px+ vertical hit area, meeting accessibility baselines. Section headers vs. flat list — Headers consume ~20px each but dramatically improve wayfinding compared to a single alphabetized or priority-sorted list. I accepted the space cost. Toasts instead of real navigation — Since this is a single-screen prototype, toasts prove clickability and state change without building a full 6-screen router hierarchy. The interaction pattern is preserved (tap → feedback), even if the destination is simulated. Truncate over wrap — Hint text uses truncate rather than wrapping. This prevents uneven row heights and maintains visual rhythm, at the cost of potentially hiding very long strings. Given the controlled content ("Visa •• 4821"), this is safe. Logout as icon, not row — Placing logout in the header separates the destructive action from navigational content. Trade-off: it's slightly less discoverable than a red "Log out" row, but it prevents accidental taps while scrolling and keeps the list focused on settings, not actions. The overall intent was to make the screen feel like a finished native retail app — dense enough to be useful, structured enough to be scannable, and compact enough to live comfortably inside the device frame with zero overflow.
