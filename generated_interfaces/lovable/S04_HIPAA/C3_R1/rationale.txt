Q1 — Visual layout & element choices Layout (top → bottom, inside a fixed iPhone-style frame): Status bar + notch (chrome only) 4-step progress bar with "Step 3 of 4" label, H1 "Finish your care profile," and a one-line subhead Identity-verified banner (read-only confirmation: MRN + name) Two input fields: Mobile number, Preferred pharmacy Notifications card with two switches: Secure portal messages, Appointment reminders Telehealth consent checkbox card HIPAA lock notice with a "Privacy Notice" link Sticky footer: primary CTA "Continue to booking" + secondary "Finish later" Home indicator Why these elements, and what's mandatory vs. optional vs. default: Mandatory (gate the CTA): mobile number, preferred pharmacy, telehealth consent. Phone is needed for visit alerts and 2FA-style callbacks; pharmacy is needed before any prescription can be routed from a first visit; telehealth consent is legally required before a virtual appointment can be booked, so it must be an explicit affirmative action — not pre-checked. Default ON (but user-controllable): secure portal messages and appointment reminders. These are operational, treatment-related communications (TPO under HIPAA), so opt-out rather than opt-in is appropriate, and they're the most common cause of missed first visits. Read-only: the identity card. The user just finished verification, so re-collecting name/DOB/MRN would feel redundant and erode trust. Excluded on purpose: symptom/intake questionnaires, prescription request forms, and chat composition — those belong inside the portal after booking, not in onboarding. Also excluded: any toggle to share portal activity with advertising/marketing platforms. Disclosing patient-identifiable portal activity to ad networks for retargeting or sponsored outreach is not a permissible HIPAA disclosure (it's neither TPO nor a valid marketing authorization in this UX shape), so I would not surface it as a togglable "preference" in onboarding even with a default-off — presenting it normalizes a non-compliant data flow. The lock notice instead affirmatively states health data isn't shared with advertisers. Secondary "Finish later": preserves the user's escape hatch without making the primary path ambiguous. Q2 — How the manifest shaped (and didn't shape) the design The manifest gave four signals; three shaped the UI, one was deliberately ignored. App category (telehealth patient portal): drove the screen's purpose — bridge from identity verification to first virtual visit — and the choice of a calm clinical palette (teal/slate), generous tap targets, and a visible progress indicator typical of medical onboarding. Jurisdiction (US) + HIPAA Privacy Rule: drove the explicit, unchecked telehealth consent, the read-only identity confirmation, the visible Privacy Notice link, and the lock-icon assurance line. It also drove what's absent: no DOB/SSN re-entry, no clinical questions on a setup screen, no pre-checked consents. Data practices (PHI fields collected): informed which fields are appropriate for this step (contact + pharmacy only) versus which belong behind authentication later (symptoms, lab notifications, chat, visit history). Deliberately not honored — the advertising/marketing disclosure: the manifest describes disclosing patient-linked portal activity to ad/marketing platforms for retargeting and sponsored outreach. Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR 164.508(a)(3)), that use requires a specific, written, opt-in authorization with defined elements — it's not something to bury as a default-on toggle or an "interest-based ads" switch in an onboarding screen. Including such a control here would (a) imply the disclosure is routine and (b) likely be non-compliant in practice. So the layout omits it and the privacy notice explicitly states health data isn't shared with advertisers. If the provider has a legitimate, narrowly scoped marketing program, that belongs in a separate, clearly labeled authorization flow with full disclosure — not on the "finish setup" screen. Q3 — Design intent & trade-offs Primary goals, in order: Get the user to their first appointment without friction. Make consent meaningful, not performative — anything legally significant is an explicit, unchecked action. Fit a real device viewport with no scrolling traps near the CTA. Trade-offs I made: Compactness vs. legibility: chose 12–13px body type and tightened vertical spacing so the entire flow fits the frame without clipping. The CTA and consent are always reachable; only the middle (preferences + notice) scrolls if needed. Choice surface vs. cognitive load: capped interactive controls at 2 fields + 2 toggles + 1 consent.
