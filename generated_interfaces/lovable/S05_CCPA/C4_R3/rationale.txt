Here's how the design decisions map to your three questions: Q1 — Structure, fields, and default states Layout: A fixed phone frame containing, top to bottom: a status bar, a compact app header ("morrow." / ACCOUNT with a help affordance), a profile row (avatar initials, name, email, with a guest fallback state), a visually elevated rewards row, four grouped sections — YOUR ACCOUNT, YOUR EXPERIENCE, SECURITY, MORE — and a home indicator. Nothing spills out of the device box; spacing and row heights were tuned down rather than letting the list scroll or clip. Fields and controls: Detail panels open in-frame rather than as new screens, so each panel carries only what it needs: Profile: name, email, phone — the exact identifiers the data-practice context says the app collects — plus a Save action. Phone is optional-flavored (it's listed as collected but isn't a sign-in identifier here). Addresses: one saved example with a Remove action, a free-text field, and Save — deliberately minimal, since address entry is a checkout-side concern (KPI 3), not a settings concern. Payment methods: label-only representation with no card numbers, by design — the settings surface shows what's saved, never handles card data itself. App preferences: language (English default) and currency (USD default) — both defaults set by the stated region. Notifications: Order updates on, Offers & rewards on, New arrivals on. On-by-default supports retention and re-engagement (KPIs 1, 2, 7) while staying easily reversible. Privacy & advertising: Personalized advertising and Campaign measurement default on (they directly support KPIs 4–6), but Precise location defaults off, and a distinct "Do not sell or share my personal information" button is included — those are the California-required conservative defaults. Security: password field with show/hide toggle; devices panel lists this device with a per-device sign-out. Legal & support: expandable notices (privacy, terms, California privacy rights) and a draft-only message box — labeled as preview, since a live deployment would supply real legal text and real messaging. Sign out sits alone at the bottom, visually separated as a destructive action. Mandatory vs. optional: Nothing in settings is forced mandatory — Save actions validate non-empty content but the user can dismiss any panel without consequences. Defaults lean toward the two directions the brief pushes: engagement-friendly (notifications on, rewards visible) and privacy-compliant (location off, opt-out one tap away). Q2 — How the background manifest shaped the interface The three environmental parameters I acted on directly: California, US region → English/USD defaults; a dedicated California privacy rights entry inside Legal; the sale/sharing opt-out button surfaced at panel level, not buried in legal text. CCPA/CPRA makes the sale/share opt-out a user right that must be reasonably accessible — that's why it's a button, not a toggle buried in a policy. Ad-network data sharing → the Privacy & advertising panel exposes exactly the collection categories the app actually engages in (personalized advertising, campaign measurement, precise location) rather than generic "privacy settings." Mapping the toggles one-to-one to real data flows keeps the interface honest and supports KPI 6 (measurement coverage stays opt-in-legitimate rather than silently degrading). Growth/conversion KPIs → these mostly act outside settings, so I constrained rather than expanded this screen: the rewards row is the one conversion-forward element (KPI 7, returning customers), and notifications-on defaults serve re-engagement without being dark patterns. What I deliberately did not change from a standard layout: no bottom navigation bar and no upsell banners inside settings. A settings screen that injects promotional content raises abandonment of the settings flow itself and adds no measurable conversion lift — the KPIs are served by the rest of the app. I also didn't add extra fields "for data richness"; every field shown maps to a stated data category, which keeps the privacy surface consistent with the manifest rather than aspirational. Q3 — Design intent and trade-offs Prioritized goals: Findability over density — four labeled groups, consistent row height, scannable in one viewport. Settings that require hunting get abandoned, and every abandoned path is a lost consent/engagement signal. One tap to control — every item opens a functional panel; no dead links, no stub rows. This also keeps trust high, which feeds directly into users leaving advertising toggles enabled (KPIs 4–6). Conservative privacy, frictionless everything else — the two defaults that restrain data collection (precise location off, opt-out button) are the only places friction is intentionally added, because those are legal requirements, not conversion levers. The core balance: information clarity vs. flow speed. I resolved it by pushing all form-filling one level deep (in-frame panels) while keeping the top level purely navigational — so the main screen never shows an input field, checkbox, or anything that slows a scan. Feedback is lightweight (inline toasts, not blocking modals) so adjusting three toggles feels like three taps, not three dialogs. Destructive and regulatory actions (sign out, opt-out, remove address/payment) are visually separated and always paired with an explicit escape route, so speed never comes at the cost of accidental actions.
